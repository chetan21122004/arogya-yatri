What Language Barriers Should I Expect in Indian Hospitals?

An honest guide to language in Indian hospitals for international patients — what to expect, where gaps exist, and how Sano's 120+ language support ensures nothing gets lost in translation.

Sano Healthcare & Tourism · July 2026

Language is one of the quieter anxieties in medical tourism — rarely the headline concern, but always there underneath.

The worry isn't about menus or directions. It's about informed consent. Medication instructions. A nurse explaining what a drip is for at 2am. A doctor describing what they found during surgery. These are moments where miscommunication isn't inconvenient — it's dangerous.

Here's what the reality looks like in Indian hospitals, honestly.

English: Better Than Most Patients Expect

India has one of the largest English-speaking populations in the world. English is a co-official government language, the medium of instruction at every medical school, and the working language of clinical documentation across all accredited hospitals.

At NABH-accredited hospitals with international patient departments, you can expect:

  • Doctors — fluent English, often with postgraduate training conducted entirely in English
  • Senior nurses — comfortable clinical communication in English
  • International patient coordinators — English as a primary working language
  • Medical records, consent forms, discharge summaries — all in English

For patients whose first language is English — from the US, UK, Australia, Nigeria, Kenya, or anywhere else — the clinical experience at an accredited hospital is largely seamless.

Where Gaps Can Appear

English fluency varies by role.

Junior nursing staff and ward attendants may have limited conversational English — enough for routine tasks, not always enough for nuanced questions about how you're feeling or what you need.

Outside the hospital — pharmacists, transport, local shops — English proficiency drops significantly. Normal for any country, but worth knowing if you're managing your own logistics during recovery.

None of this is unique to India. But at home you have a support network around you. In India, that support needs to be built into your travel arrangements from the start.

If English Isn't Your First Language

This is where language becomes a clinical matter, not just a comfort one.

A French-speaking patient from Côte d'Ivoire who doesn't fully understand the anaesthetist's pre-operative questions. An Arabic-speaking patient from Oman who can't read the medication instructions on their discharge prescription. A Portuguese-speaking patient from Brazil who misunderstands their physiotherapy protocol.

These aren't edge cases — they're common situations. And at accredited hospitals, while Arabic and French often have some in-house support given patient volumes from the Middle East and West Africa, coverage beyond these languages varies significantly between facilities.

Practical Steps to Protect Yourself

Regardless of language, these habits reduce communication risk:

Write things down in consultations. Don't rely on memory. Bring a notebook, or use your phone. Ask your doctor to write down medication names, doses, and what to watch for.

Ask for written instructions. Medication schedules, wound care, physiotherapy exercises — written, before you leave any appointment.

Repeat back what you've understood. Especially for consent and post-operative instructions. "So what I'm hearing is..." is good clinical communication, not being difficult.

Confirm medication generic names. Brand names vary between countries. Your home pharmacist needs the generic name — confirm it before you leave India.

Have your Sano coordinator present for key conversations. They're a communication bridge, not just a logistics manager — a second set of ears, or an interpreter, at any appointment where you want one.
