What Are the Risks of Getting Surgery in India as a Foreigner?

An honest look at the real risks of surgery in India for international patients — and the specific ones that are different from surgery at home. What to know, what to check, and what to stop worrying about.

Sano Healthcare & Tourism · August 2026

Every surgery carries risk. That's true whether you're in Mumbai, Manchester, or Minnesota.

The question worth asking isn't "is surgery in India risky?" — it's "which risks are the same as at home, which are different, and which are specific to being a foreigner?" Because conflating them leads to either false reassurance or unnecessary fear, and neither serves you.

Here's an honest breakdown.

Risks That Are the Same Everywhere

These are surgical risks that exist regardless of where you're treated — and that your surgeon should discuss with you before any procedure, anywhere in the world:

  • Anaesthetic reactions
  • Bleeding and haematoma
  • Infection at the surgical site
  • Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism
  • Nerve damage
  • Implant failure or rejection (for relevant procedures)
  • Unsatisfactory outcome — the procedure delivers less relief than expected

At NABH and JCI-accredited hospitals in India, these risks are managed with the same protocols used in Western hospitals — sterile operating theatres, pre-operative screening, anaesthesia monitoring, anticoagulation management, and post-operative infection surveillance. Accreditation isn't a guarantee against complications, but it's independent verification that a hospital has the systems to minimise and manage them.

Risks That Are Genuinely Different When Travelling for Surgery

These are the ones worth paying attention to — because they're specific to the medical tourism context, not just surgery in general.

The flight home.

Long-haul flights after surgery carry real DVT risk, particularly after joint replacement, cardiac surgery, and major abdominal procedures. This isn't unique to India — it applies to surgery anywhere you have to fly home from. The mitigation is clear: don't fly before your surgeon gives written fit-to-fly clearance, wear compression socks for the full journey, and move the aisle every 60–90 minutes. Full recovery and fit-to-fly guide →

The handover gap.

The transition from your Indian care team to your home doctor is where things can fall through. If your GP doesn't have your operative notes, implant details, and discharge instructions in a usable format, your follow-up care is improvised rather than planned. This is manageable — but only if someone is actively managing it. How Sano handles follow-up care →

Complications developing after you've returned home.

A wound that looks fine when you leave India may behave differently two weeks later. You're no longer near the surgical team. If you develop a complication at home — infection, implant concern, unexpected pain — your local doctor is working from records, not direct knowledge of your case. Having a named remote contact at your Indian hospital and a Sano coordinator still available makes this manageable. Having neither doesn't.

Choosing the wrong hospital or surgeon.

This is the highest-stakes risk in medical tourism, and the one most within your control. A surgeon at an accredited hospital with high case volume for your specific procedure is a fundamentally different proposition from a surgeon at an unaccredited facility offering an attractive price. The gap between good and poor outcomes in surgery is largely explained by this. How to find a legitimate hospital in India →

Risks People Worry About That Are Usually Overstated

"Infection from Indian hospitals."

This concern is based on a generalisation that doesn't hold at accredited facilities. Infection control at NABH and JCI-accredited hospitals is independently audited and meets international standards. The risk of surgical site infection is not meaningfully higher at these hospitals than at comparable private hospitals in Europe or North America.

"The language barrier will cause a mistake."

At international patient departments, English is the working language of clinical care. For patients who don't speak English, Sano's multilingual support — covering 120+ languages through Ewan Business Solutions — means clinical conversations are conducted in yours. A misunderstood medication instruction is a risk; it's also a preventable one. Language guide for Indian hospitals →

"Something will go wrong and I'll be alone."

This fear is real, but it's about isolation rather than clinical risk. Being far from home when something goes wrong is frightening. Having a Sano coordinator who stays with your case, a named contact at the hospital, and a remote line to your surgical team after you return home changes this picture substantially.

"The doctors aren't as good."

Senior surgeons at India's accredited hospitals are trained to the same standard as their counterparts in the US or UK — many did fellowships at Western institutions. In high-volume specialties, they often have more hands-on experience. Read the full doctor quality guide →

How to Reduce the Risks That Are Within Your Control

Choose an accredited hospital, verified independently. NABH status at nabh.co, JCI status at jointcommissioninternational.org. Don't accept self-certification.

Know your surgeon before you travel. Name, qualifications, procedure-specific case volume. If a hospital won't tell you who is operating, that's the answer.

Plan follow-up care before you leave India. Remote consultation schedule, medical records in transferable format, named contact for post-discharge concerns. Sano's post-treatment coordination →

Don't fly before your fit-to-fly clearance. Written, from your surgeon, specific to your actual flight duration.

Travel with a companion. For practical help in recovery and for someone to manage communication if you can't. How to bring a family member →

Work with a facilitator who stays available after you leave. The risk of abandonment post-discharge is real in medical tourism. Sano's involvement doesn't stop at the airport.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is surgery in India safe for foreigners?

At NABH and JCI-accredited hospitals, yes — with the same caveats that apply to surgery anywhere. Accreditation is independently verified. The risks specific to travelling for surgery are manageable with proper planning.

What if there's a complication during surgery?

Accredited hospitals carry institutional liability coverage and have defined complication management protocols. Additional care required within the same surgical episode is handled as part of your treatment agreement. Discuss this explicitly with your hospital and Sano before travelling.

What if I'm not happy with the result?

Dissatisfaction with a surgical outcome — as distinct from a complication — is handled through the hospital's patient relations process and, where appropriate, your treatment agreement terms. Sano supports patients through this process when it arises.

Is there any legal recourse if something goes wrong?

India has medical negligence law. Pursuing legal recourse from another country is complex and slow — this is a genuine limitation of medical tourism that's worth acknowledging honestly. It's one of the reasons choosing a reputable, accredited hospital is more important than finding the cheapest option.

The Honest Summary

The risks of surgery in India are real — but so are the risks of surgery anywhere. The ones that are specific to being a foreigner are mostly about logistics and transitions: the flight home, the handover to your home doctor, the gap between finishing treatment and being back in a familiar system.

Every one of those risks is addressable with planning. None of them is a reason not to go, if the treatment is right for your situation.
